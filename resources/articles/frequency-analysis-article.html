Details Behind Frequency Analysis

Introduction to Frequency Analysis

Frequency analysis is a cryptanalysis method used to study the frequency distribution or count of letters/digits or groups of letters/digits in a given ciphertext. Frequency analysis can be used as an aid in cracking ciphertexts using classic cipher encryptions. You may be wondering, how is a method like this possible? Well, for every language, differing for the most part, individual or grouped letters appear more frequently than others. The letter "E" appears most frequently (about 12.7% of the time), while the letters "X," "J", "Q", and "Z" appear less frequently in the English alphabet. Other common letters that appear frequently in the English alphabet, are "T", "A", and "O". Substitution ciphers (like Caesar, Vigenère) encrypt text by replacing original letters with different letters. Despite the change or shift in letters, the frequency of each letter remains equivalent in the ciphertext. By keeping track of how often specific letters show up in the encrypted text, it can be easy to spot clues and patterns, and decipher the message from there. Please refer to the letter frequency chart below, as this will give you an idea of how often or number of times each letter frequently appears in the English language.

Remember, too, frequency analysis doesn't have to rely only on individual characters. Frequency analysis can resort to analyzing grouped letters/symbols, such as bigrams(combination of 2 letters), trigrams (combination of three letters), and so on - collectively known as n-grams.

Example: Breaking a Caesar Cipher Using Frequency Analysis

Scenario: Bob came across a ciphertext that reads "WKH SDVVZRUG LV VHYHQ GRQW WHOO DQBRQH", and it is known to be encrypted using a simple substitution cipher. Bob decides to use frequency analysis to try and crack the ciphertext. The following steps he decides to follow are:

  • Count the frequency of each letter: H(5), V(4), Q(4), W(3), R(3), D(2), G(2), O(2), K(1), S(1), Z(1), U(1), L(1), Y(1), B(1)
  • From there he compares with known frequency distribution in English

Since "E" is the most frequent letter in the English alphabet, and "H" is the most frequent letter in the ciphertext, let's assume H corresponds to "E". From "H" to "E" (counting backwards), it is 3 positions back. Just note that this method won’t always work. Guessing and comparing with other common, frequent letters in the English alphabet might be necessary. Going back to our example, for every letter, we can substitute with a "-3" in the alphabet. Counting down 3 in the alphabet, following this pattern, "V" becomes "S", "Q" becomes "N", "W" becomes "T", "R" becomes "O", "D" becomes "A", "G" becomes "D", "O" becomes "L", and so on. If Bob keeps following the same procedure, Bob will figure out that the message was encrypted using the Caesar cipher with a shift of 3, and that the plaintext message was "THE PASSWORD IS SEVEN DONT TELL ANYONE". When is Frequency Analysis Effective?

Frequency analysis is effective when plaintext is encrypted using a simple substitution cipher and when the ciphertext is long enough. However, the method becomes ineffective when plaintext is encrypted using modern cryptographic algorithms, when the ciphertext is too short, and when multiple letters or characters are substituted at once.
